Thesis: CSR reports exist to satisfy legal requirements, not to inform. Research treating them as real signals produces contradictions and should not drive investor or policy decisions.

Problem 1

Reports check a legal box, not designed to communicate

Problem 2

Institutional and retail investors process information in completely different ways

Problem 3

Two contradicting theories applied together make results meaningless

Who asked for these reports and why

Why companies started publishing

  • Investors needed ESG data to model financial risk
  • Governments pushed for standardized formats
  • 10 of 18 companies told GAO: legal obligation is the main reason

What readability reveals

  • Hard to read reports: actual CSR results stop affecting decisions
  • If communication were the goal, clarity would not be optional

Gao et al. (2023): Same company, same CSR results. Easy version: high investor estimates, statistically significant. Hard version: estimates near zero, not significant. Only the writing changed.

Not all investors work the same way

Institutional investors

  • 12 of 14: sole goal is risk assessment (GAO, 2020)
  • Full teams, multiple data sources
  • Their behavior tells us nothing about regular investors

Retail investors

  • React to reputation and third-party verification (Ernst et al., 2025)
  • Decide by feeling, not analysis
  • No tools to evaluate complex reports on their own

Yang et al. (2026), China 2007 to 2019: First-time publishers saw +1.5% stock return. Long-term publishers saw +1.1% via a different mechanism. Short-term publishers saw no reaction. Mixing all three produces an average that describes none of them.

The theories contradict each other

Theory When it works The problem
Signaling theory First-time disclosures only Cannot explain ongoing disclosure reactions
Affect-as-information theory Long-running disclosures only Cannot explain first-time reactions
Both used together Most existing studies Results describe nothing specific

Reputation beats content

Ernst et al. (2025): investors who like the company assume the report is genuine. Most studies measure brand image, not disclosure effects.

Writing style beats facts

Gao et al. (2023): identical CSR performance, opposite investor reactions based only on how the document was written.

Common objections

1
Big investors use these reports, so they must work
They use them for risk only. GAO found even these investors needed extra sources due to gaps. If the most equipped users find them insufficient, the argument collapses.
2
Standardizing the format would fix everything
GAO found companies using the same GRI framework still produced inconsistent data. Standardized compliance paperwork is still compliance paperwork.
3
Every new field has inconsistencies early on
These are not growing pains. Mixing investor types and applying contradicting theories are design flaws. More studies with the same approach will not fix them.

Conclusion

CSR reports check a legal box. Research has not accounted for who is reading, how the document is written, or how long the company has been publishing.

Reports serve compliance

If communication were the goal, clarity would be the first requirement.

Research misses investor differences

Mixing institutional and retail investors produces findings that represent nobody.

Format and reputation win

Both matter more than report content. Most studies never control for either.

For policymakers

Do not design reform based on this research. It cannot tell you what actually improves investor decisions.

For future research

Investor type, disclosure history, readability, and reputation must be built into study design from the start.
