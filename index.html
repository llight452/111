Thesis: CSR reports satisfy regulatory requirements, not inform anyone. Research treating them as reliable signals produces contradictions that should not drive real decisions by investors, policymakers, or consumers.

Problem 1

Reports function as compliance, not communication

Problem 2

Institutional and retail investors are fundamentally different

Problem 3

Signaling and affect-as-information theories cannot both apply at same time

CSR Reports as Formal Documents

Why these reports exist

  • Institutional investors needed ESG data for risk modeling
  • Policy pushed for standardization
  • 10 of 18 companies: legal obligation is primary driver (GAO, 2020)

What readability reveals

  • Low readability: CSR performance effect becomes insignificant (Gao et al., 2023)
  • Eye-tracking: low readability pulls attention without improving comprehension
  • If communication mattered, clarity would not be optional

Gao et al. (2023) experiment result: High readability + positive CSR → earnings estimate 2.09, significant (p<0.01). Low readability + positive CSR → earnings estimate 0.50, not significant (p=0.403). Same performance, opposite results.

The Institutional vs. Retail Investor Problem

Institutional investors

  • 12 of 14: main reason is risk assessment (GAO, 2020)
  • Dedicated teams, multiple data sources
  • Respond to risk-relevant data points
  • Cannot represent retail behavior

Retail investors

  • Respond to reputation and third-party assurance (Ernst et al., 2025)
  • Judgment is perceptual, not analytical
  • Use shortcuts, lack tools to evaluate reports

Yang et al. (2026) — China 2007–2019: First-time disclosures → +1.5% CAR via signaling. Long-duration continuous → +1.1% CAR via affective identification. Short-duration continuous → no significant reaction. Mixing all three in one sample without controls produces meaningless averages.

Contradictions in Existing Research

Theory Applies to Problem
Signaling Theory First-time disclosures only Cannot explain continuous disclosure reactions
Affect-as-Information Theory Long-duration continuous only Cannot explain first-time disclosure reactions
Both used together Most existing studies Findings describe nothing precisely

Reputation > Report content

Ernst et al. (2025): favorable reputation affects investor attribution of motives. Studies claiming to measure disclosure effects are often measuring brand image instead.

Format > Performance

Gao et al. (2023): same CSR performance, opposite results depending on readability. Findings cannot transfer across companies with different writing styles.

Counterarguments

CA1
Institutional investors use reports → they work
Only for risk. One narrow function. GAO found most still sought additional disclosures because existing ones had gaps. If best-equipped users find them insufficient, the argument falls apart.
CA2
Standardization would fix the problem
GAO: even companies using same GRI framework had inconsistencies. GRI does not always specify calculation methods. Standardized compliance paperwork is still compliance paperwork.
CA3
Inconsistency is normal in developing fields
These contradictions are structural, not early-stage. Conflating investor populations and applying incompatible theories are design problems. More studies using same approach will not fix them.

Conclusion

CSR reports check a legal box. Research treating them as reliable signals has not accounted for conditions under which signals actually work, for whom, and under what circumstances.

Reports serve compliance

Readability is optional. Clarity would be priority if communication was the real goal.

Research misses investor differences

Institutional and retail investors process information completely differently. Mixing them produces findings that describe neither.

Format and reputation dominate

Both matter more than actual content. Most studies do not control for either.

For policymakers

Do not design reform based on this literature. It cannot tell you what actually improves decision-making across the full range of investors.

For future research

Investor type, disclosure history, readability, and reputation must be fundamental design variables, not afterthoughts.
