CSR Reports:
Paperwork, Not Information

Why researchers and investors cannot trust what companies write about themselves

Andrei Volodin

Research Paper Presentation

Main argument: CSR reports exist to satisfy legal requirements, not to communicate. Research that treats them as real signals produces contradictions and should not guide investors or policymakers.

Problem 1

These reports are written to check a legal box, not to actually inform the reader

Problem 2

Big institutional investors and small retail investors work completely differently — you cannot study them together

Problem 3

Two theories used in research directly contradict each other, but most studies apply both at the same time

Who asked for these reports — and why?

Why companies started publishing

  • Large investors needed ESG data to model financial risk
  • Governments pushed for standardized reporting
  • 10 out of 18 companies told the GAO that legal obligation is the main driver

What readability shows us

  • When a report is hard to read, the company's CSR performance stops affecting investor estimates
  • Eye-tracking studies show that complicated text grabs attention but does not improve understanding
  • If these reports were meant to inform people, readability would be a priority — not an accident
Key finding from Gao et al. (2023): Same company, same CSR performance. Easy to read version: investors gave high earnings estimates (p less than 0.01, statistically significant). Hard to read version: estimates dropped to near zero and were not statistically significant (p = 0.40). The only thing that changed was how the document was written.

Not all investors are the same

Institutional investors (big funds)

  • 12 of 14 told GAO their only goal is risk assessment
  • Have entire teams dedicated to reading these reports
  • Use many data sources at once, not just the company's own report
  • Their behavior cannot represent what regular investors do

Retail investors (regular people)

  • Respond to company reputation and whether reports are verified by a third party
  • Make decisions based on feeling and perception, not detailed analysis
  • Do not have the tools or time to evaluate technical reports on their own
Yang et al. (2026) — China, 2007 to 2019: Companies publishing for the first time saw a +1.5% stock return boost. Companies that published for many years in a row saw +1.1% — but through a completely different psychological mechanism. Companies that published for just a short period saw no reaction at all. Mixing these three groups together in one study produces an average that describes none of them accurately.

The theories contradict each other

Theory When it works The problem
Signaling theory First-time disclosure only Cannot explain why people react to ongoing disclosures
Affect-as-information theory Long-running disclosures only Cannot explain why people react to first-time announcements
Both theories used together Most existing studies The findings describe nothing specific — they are averages of incompatible mechanisms

Reputation matters more than content

Ernst et al. (2025): investors with a positive impression of a company automatically assume the company's motives for publishing are genuine. Studies claiming to measure disclosure effects are often actually measuring brand image.

Writing style matters more than facts

Gao et al. (2023): identical CSR performance produced opposite investor reactions depending only on how the document was written. Results from one company cannot transfer to another with a different writing style.

Common objections — and why they do not hold

1
Institutional investors use these reports, so they must work
They use them for one narrow purpose only: risk. The GAO found that even the most sophisticated investors had to seek additional disclosures because existing reports had too many gaps. If the best-resourced users find them insufficient, the argument falls apart.
2
Standardizing the format would solve the problem
The GAO found that companies using the exact same GRI reporting framework still produced inconsistent results, because GRI does not specify how to calculate most things. Making compliance paperwork more uniform does not make it genuinely informative.
3
Every new research field has inconsistencies at first
These contradictions are not growing pains — they are structural problems. Mixing investor types and applying two incompatible theories are design flaws. Producing more studies using the same flawed approach will not fix them.

What does this all mean?

Bottom line: CSR reports check a legal box. Research that treats them as real informational signals has not accounted for who is reading, how the document is written, or how long the company has been publishing.

Reports serve compliance

Readability is optional in current practice. If communication were the real goal, clarity would be the first requirement — not an afterthought.

Research misses investor differences

Institutional and retail investors process the same information through completely different mental processes. Studies that mix them produce averages that represent nobody.

Format and reputation dominate

Both matter more than the actual content of a report. Most studies do not control for either of these factors.

For policymakers

Do not design reform based on this literature. The current research cannot tell you what actually improves decision-making for the full range of investors.

For future researchers

Investor type, disclosure history, readability, and company reputation must be core design variables from the start — not notes added at the end of a paper.
